Lithography: resist track and EUV scanner

Lithography prints a chip's pattern onto the wafer, one layer at a time. A track coats the wafer with a light-sensitive film called resist, and the scanner projects a pattern onto it with 13.5 nm extreme ultraviolet (EUV) light, 4 times smaller than the mask. Press Play to follow one wafer through both machines.

What happens

  1. Coat and bake. The track spins the wafer while a nozzle drops resist on its centre; spinning spreads it into a thin, even film. A hot plate then drives out the solvent (the soft bake).
  2. Make the light. In the source vessel, a 30 kW CO2 laser hits 50,000 tin droplets a second. A weak pre-pulse flattens each droplet and the main pulse turns it into a plasma at about 220,000 °C, which glows at 13.5 nm. A curved collector mirror focuses that light through a small opening, the intermediate focus.
  3. Shape it and bounce it off the mask. EUV light is absorbed by air and by glass, so the scanner works in vacuum and uses only mirrors. The illuminator's facet mirrors turn the light into an even slit, which falls on the reticle: a mirror-like mask with the circuit pattern in an absorbing layer, held face down.
  4. Project it 4× smaller. Six projection mirrors (NA 0.33) image the lit slit of the reticle onto the wafer, 4 times smaller. The reticle and the wafer scan past the slit in opposite directions, the reticle 4 times faster, so one sweep prints a whole 26 × 33 mm field. The stage then steps to the next field: 96 of them per wafer. Meanwhile a second wafer stage maps the next wafer's height and alignment marks, so the two stages swap and exposure never waits for measurement.
  5. Bake and develop. Back in the track, a post-exposure bake finishes the chemistry where the light fell, and a developer puddle dissolves the exposed (or unexposed) resist. The pattern now stands in the resist, ready for etch.

Why it matters

  • Shorter light prints smaller features. Moving from 193 nm to 13.5 nm let a single exposure print layers that once needed several deep-UV exposures and extra mask steps.
  • Every optic is a mirror coated with up to 100 alternating ultra-thin layers; a single layer would reflect about 1%. Losses multiply at each bounce, which is why source power sets the scanner's speed.
  • The NXE:3800E reached 220 wafers an hour, 37% more than the NXE:3600D, with layer-to-layer overlay of 0.9 nm between matched machines.
  • The next step, High-NA EUV (NA 0.55), keeps the 13.5 nm light and enlarges the optics to print finer still; it has its own chapter.
