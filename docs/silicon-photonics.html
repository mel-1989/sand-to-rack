Switch package with optical engines and fibre

Co-packaged optics

A big AI cluster is held together by switches, and a large share of their power goes into moving bits from the switch chip to the optics at the front of the box. Co-packaged optics moves the optics onto the chip's own package, so a bit turns into light a few millimetres from where it was made instead of after a long trip across a circuit board.

What happens

  1. The bit leaves the switch chip. A SerDes on the ASIC sends it as a 200 Gb/s PAM4 electrical signal, four voltage levels carrying two bits a symbol, across a few millimetres of package wiring to the electronic chip of an optical engine.
  2. Laser light comes in. The light itself is made off the package, in external laser source modules at the board edge (8 lasers each, NVIDIA's design). Steady, unmodulated light reaches the engine through its own fibres. Keeping the lasers out of the hot package makes them easier to cool and to replace.
  3. A micro-ring writes the bit onto the light. On the photonic chip the light runs along a silicon waveguide past a ring a few tens of micrometres across. The driver pushes charge in and out of the ring, shifting its resonance; when the ring is in tune it pulls light out of the waveguide, when it is out of tune the light passes. Four drive levels give four light levels: PAM4 in light.
  4. Out on the fibre. A glass fibre attach block couples the waveguide into single-mode fibre. At 200 Gb/s a lane, copper cable reaches about 1 m; single-mode fibre reaches 500 m to 2 km (IEEE 802.3dj).
  5. A photodiode turns it back. At the far end, here the same package through a loopback fibre, light falls on a photodiode (typically germanium) on another engine's photonic chip and becomes a small current.
  6. Back to electrical. A transimpedance amplifier on the electronic chip turns that current into a voltage, and the switch's receiving SerDes recovers the bit.
  7. Compare a pluggable. In a switch with pluggable transceivers the same bit first crosses the board to a module in the front panel, where a DSP cleans it up and an externally modulated laser writes it onto light. NVIDIA's GTC 2025 example, for a 1.6 Tb/s port on its Spectrum-X Photonics switch, put that at 30 W and 22 dB of electrical loss, against 9 W and 4 dB co-packaged. The figure uses those numbers as representative.

Why it matters

  • Network power grows with every GPU added. Cutting a port from 30 W to 9 W frees power for compute, and NVIDIA also counts 4× fewer lasers, because one external laser feeds many lanes.
  • Micro-rings are tiny compared with the Mach-Zehnder modulators that silicon-photonics pluggables use (many pluggables use an externally modulated laser instead, as in step 7), so many lanes fit on a small chip right beside the switch; the price is that a ring's resonance drifts with temperature and has to be held in tune, typically by a small heater under control.
  • TSMC's COUPE stacks the electronic chip on the photonic chip (SoIC-X), so driver and modulator are microns apart. TSMC's April 2024 roadmap called for qualifying it in small pluggables in 2025 and in CoWoS packages as co-packaged optics in 2026.
  • The next step is optics on the accelerator package itself, replacing copper for GPU-to-GPU links. The Ethernet sibling, Spectrum-X Photonics, already puts 32 engines of 3.2 Tb/s around one switch chip.
