Multi-wire saw

Slicing and polishing wafers

A grown crystal is a long cylinder of single-crystal silicon. To become wafers it is cut into blocks, ground round, glued to a beam and pushed through a web of fine wire that cuts it into hundreds of slices at once. Each slice is then rounded at the edge, etched and polished on both faces until it is flat and clean enough to build chips on. Press Play to watch it happen.

What happens

  1. Grind and notch. The crystal is cut into blocks and ground round to just over the finished 300 mm; the edge grind later trims each wafer to 300 ± 0.1 mm. A groove ground along its length becomes the 1.0 mm notch on every wafer, marking the ⟨110⟩ crystal direction so later tools can line the wafer up. In the figure the notch is drawn about 14 times deeper than it really is, so it can be seen.
  2. Mount. The block is glued to a sacrificial beam on a work plate, and the plate is clamped into the saw's feed head. The wire finishes each cut in the beam, so the slices stay hanging from it.
  3. Slice. One steel wire, 0.14 mm thick, is wound hundreds of times round grooved guide rollers to make a web. It runs at up to 1,000 m/min while slurry, an abrasive carried in fluid, flows onto it, and the head feeds the block down through the web at 0.01–2.5 mm/min. Every wire cuts at once. Diamond wire, which carries its grit fixed to the wire and cuts faster, has become the main way to slice silicon. Slurry leaves a smoother surface with less damage underneath and chips the edges less, and it is still widely used for large-diameter ingots like this one.
  4. Edge grind. A grooved profile wheel rounds each wafer's sharp edge so it does not chip in later handling, and in the same pass grinds it to its exact diameter.
  5. Etch. First, off the station drawn here, lapping takes off roughly 10 µm from each face to make them parallel and remove saw marks; many 300 mm lines grind both faces instead. Then an alkaline or acid etch, the spin etcher in the figure, strips the damaged layer that is left.
  6. Double-side polish. Wafers sit in thin toothed carriers between two padded platens. The carriers turn like planet gears while slurry polishes both faces at once. A last single-side touch polish usually follows to give the front face its final finish.
  7. Clean and inspect. After cleaning, each wafer is measured and checked for particles before it ships: 300 ± 0.1 mm across and 775 ± 25 µm thick. One supplier's catalogue grade allows up to 200 particles of 0.2 µm or larger; wafers for leading-edge chips are held to far smaller particles and lower counts.

Why it is done this way

  • One pass of the saw cuts hundreds of wafers at once, and each cut is only a little wider than the 0.14 mm wire, so little of the costly crystal turns to dust.
  • The notch replaced the flat edge of smaller wafers. It wastes far less area and still tells every tool which way the crystal faces.
  • 300 mm wafers are polished on both sides at once. A double-side polisher makes the two faces flat and parallel together, which lithography needs across the whole wafer.
  • Each step leaves a little damage that the next one removes: saw marks, then lapping marks, then a chemically damaged layer, until the surface is a near-perfect crystal.
