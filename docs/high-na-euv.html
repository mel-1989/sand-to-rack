Anamorphic 0.55 NA projection optics

High-NA EUV

High-NA EUV keeps the same 13.5 nm light as today's EUV scanners but collects it in a much wider cone, which lets it print features about 1.7 times smaller in one exposure. The price is a bigger, heavier, costlier machine and an exposure field half the usual size. Press Play to follow the light down to the wafer and watch two half fields become one die.

What happens

  1. Make the light. As in every EUV scanner, a CO2 laser turns tin droplets into a plasma that glows at 13.5 nm, and a collector mirror focuses it into the machine. ASML rates its EXE:5200B at 175 or more wafers an hour at 50 mJ/cm², up from 110 for the first EXE:5000.
  2. Light the reticle. Facet mirrors in the illuminator shape the light into an even slit on the reticle, which is still the standard 6-inch mask with a 104 × 132 mm pattern area.
  3. Shrink it unevenly. The projection optics are anamorphic: they shrink the pattern 4× across the slit and 8× along the scan, instead of 4× both ways. The bigger mirrors a higher NA needs make light strike the reticle at a steeper angle, where its mirror coating stops reflecting. Shrinking 8× both ways would fix that but needs a larger reticle; shrinking 8× only along the scan keeps the standard one (ASML).
  4. A wider cone. Numerical aperture is the sine of the cone's half-angle. At NA 0.55 the light arrives at up to 33.4° from vertical, against 19.3° at NA 0.33, so the image carries finer detail: 8 nm instead of 13 nm.
  5. Half fields. The 8× shrink along the scan halves the printed field to 26 × 16.5 mm, so a wafer takes twice as many exposures. The reticle stage now accelerates at 32 g and the wafer stage at 8 g (4× and 2× an NXE) to win back the time.
  6. Stitch. A die larger than a half field, such as a reticle-sized AI accelerator, is printed in two exposures from two reticles whose halves meet along a seam. Intel calls this stitching and says it lets designers keep standard masks.

Why it matters

  • One High-NA exposure can replace several low-NA exposures on the finest layers, cutting process steps, mask count and the overlay errors that come with each extra pass.
  • Intel was the first buyer. By September 2026 it reported more than 1 million wafers through its High-NA tools and volume use on select Intel 18A layers; its EXE:5200B passed acceptance at 175 wafers an hour and 0.7 nm overlay (as reported). Intel 14A is its first node designed around High-NA; in April 2025 Intel said 14A risk production would start in 2027, with volume production to follow in 2028.
  • The cost is size and speed: a field half as large means twice the exposures, so throughput depends on much faster stages and a stronger source, and a reticle-sized die must be stitched.
