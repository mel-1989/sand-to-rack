HBM stacks through the generations

Stacked memory: HBM1 to HBM4

An AI accelerator spends much of its time waiting for memory, so the memory sits right beside it as stacks of DRAM dies joined by vertical wires. Each generation of this high-bandwidth memory (HBM) has stacked more dies, run each pin faster and, with HBM4, doubled the number of pins. At the bottom of every stack sits a base die that holds the interface circuits driving the 1,024 (in HBM4, 2,048) wires to the processor across the silicon interposer. Press Play to send the height gauge along the bench, one generation at a time.

What happens

  1. HBM (2013). JEDEC's first standard stacks 4 DRAM dies on a base die and gives each stack a 1,024-bit interface at 1 Gb/s a pin: 128 GB/s and 1 GB. AMD's Fury X carried 4 of them in 2015.
  2. HBM2 (2016). JEDEC's second standard allows eight-high stacks and doubles the pin rate to 2 Gb/s: 256 GB/s and 8 GB a stack, from eight 8 Gb dies.
  3. HBM2E (2020). Still eight dies on the same 1,024-bit interface, but each die doubles to 16 Gb and the pins run at 3.6 Gb/s: 460 GB/s and 16 GB a stack in SK hynix's part.
  4. HBM3 (2022). The pin rate nearly doubles to 6.4 Gb/s (819 GB/s a stack), the channels go from 8 to 16, and stacks reach 12 dies. Twelve dies fit in the same 720 µm as eight because each die is ground thinner.
  5. HBM3E (2024). 9.6 Gb/s a pin, about 1.2 TB/s a stack, and 36 GB in a 12-high stack of 24 Gb dies. Eight-high stacks of the same dies hold 24 GB (Micron).
  6. HBM4 (2025). The interface doubles to 2,048 bits for the first time since HBM began, so 8 Gb/s a pin gives 2 TB/s a stack; in Rubin, 8 stacks deliver 22 TB/s, about 2.75 TB/s each (about 10.7 Gb/s a pin). The base die moves to a logic process (SK hynix has it made at TSMC), and the height limit rises to 775 µm. The first 12-high stacks hold 36 GB (SK hynix, Micron); the standard allows up to 16-high and 64 GB.
  7. Next: HBM4E and HBM5. 16 and then 20 dies are expected. At 16 dies (HBM4E) and above, hybrid bonding is expected to begin replacing micro-bumps; makers have so far stayed with thermo-compression and MR-MUF. In hybrid bonding, copper pads on facing dies fuse directly with no bump and no gap. There are vendor targets only so far: Samsung aims HBM4E at over 13 Gb/s a pin and about 3.25 TB/s a stack for 2027, and JEDEC is discussing a 900 to 1,000 µm height limit for 20-high HBM5.

Key innovations

  • Through-silicon vias. Copper-filled holes run straight through each thinned die, so every die in the stack reaches the base die over thousands of short vertical wires instead of long bond wires.
  • Thinner dies, same height. Grinding each DRAM die thinner is what lets 8, then 12, then 16 dies fit under one height limit, so a stack sits level with the compute die beside it under one cold plate.
  • Gap fill. SK hynix's mass reflow molded underfill (MR-MUF) floods the gaps between all dies at once; Samsung and Micron laminate a film between dies and bond each one under heat and pressure. Both fill the gap around the same micro-bumps, remove heat and keep thin dies from warping; SK hynix carries an Advanced MR-MUF into HBM4.
  • A logic base die. In HBM4 the bottom die moves to a logic process (SK hynix has it made at TSMC), with faster, smaller transistors that drive twice as many pins at lower power.
  • Hybrid bonding next. Bonding copper pad to copper pad with no bump removes the gap between dies, which saves height for 16 to 20 dies and shortens the path heat must cross.
