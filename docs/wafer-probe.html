Wafer prober and probe card

Testing: wafer probe to final test

Not every die on a finished wafer works, and packaging a bad one wastes everything stacked around it. So before the wafer is cut, a prober presses a card of microscopic springs onto each die in turn while a tester the size of a wardrobe runs it, and the result goes into a map of good and bad dies. Press play to watch one wafer go through.

What happens

  1. Load. A robot takes the wafer from its FOUP, pre-aligns it by its notch and lays it on a vacuum chuck that can be held anywhere from about -40 to +150 °C, so parts are tested cold and hot.
  2. Align. A camera looking down finds marks on the wafer and a camera on the stage looks up at the probe tips. With both positions known, the stage can put any pad under any probe, repeating to about ±1 µm.
  3. Touch down. The chuck rises until the probes touch the die's pads, then keeps going about 75 µm (cards are specified anywhere from 25 to 150 µm). The overdrive makes every one of thousands of springs press hard enough to scrub through the pad's oxide.
  4. Test. The test head, docked on the card through a ring of pogo pins, drives the die through scan, memory and functional patterns and measures currents and timing. An Advantest V93000 or Teradyne UltraFLEX class tester drives its pins at up to 32 Gb/s.
  5. Bin and step. The die is binned pass or fail (often into several speed or repair bins), the chuck drops, steps one die pitch and rises again. A full wafer of 64 large dies is a map like the one on the monitor.
  6. After the wafer. Good dies are cut out and packaged, then tested again: final test in a socket on the same kind of tester, often burn-in at raised temperature and voltage to shake out weak parts early, and for AI accelerators system-level test in a board that runs real workloads at full power. One Blackwell-class part can spend well over 20 minutes on a tester, against 30-60 s for a phone chip.

Why it matters now

  • Known good die. A GPU package carries 8 HBM stacks around the compute dies; one bad die found after assembly scraps the whole package, so every die is tested before it is bonded.
  • HBM is tested many times. DRAM wafers are probed 64-128 dies at a time, and a 12-high stack sees 3 to 12 test insertions as it is built, because one bad base die can take 16 DRAM dies with it.
  • MEMS probe cards. Lithographically made springs replaced hand-placed tungsten needles, which is what makes cards of tens of thousands of probes at fine pitch possible, and multi-site cards test several dies per touchdown.
  • Test moves left and right. More testing at the wafer (hot and cold, burn-in on the wafer) and more after packaging (system-level test) both grow, because escapes are so costly at the package and rack level.
