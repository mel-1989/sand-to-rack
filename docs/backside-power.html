Front-side vs backside power cutaway

Backside power delivery

For fifty years every chip took its power the same way as its signals: down from the top, through the whole stack of copper wiring, to the transistors at the bottom. Backside power splits the two. The wafer is flipped, its silicon ground almost away, and a separate power network is built on the back, right under the transistors. Intel ships it as PowerVia on Intel 18A; TSMC's version, Super Power Rail, comes with TSMC A16.

What happens

  1. Front end as usual. Transistors are made in the top of an ordinary 775 µm wafer, then the fine copper wiring above them. On a backside-power chip these front layers now carry only signals.
  2. Bond a carrier. A blank carrier wafer is bonded face to face onto the wiring (imec uses a SiCN-to-SiCN fusion bond at room temperature, annealed at 250 °C). From now on the carrier is what holds the chip.
  3. Flip. The pair is turned over, so the back of the original wafer faces up.
  4. Thin. Grinding, polishing and a selective etch take the silicon from 775 µm down to a few hundred nanometres, held flat to within 40 nm across the wafer.
  5. Connect. Nano-TSVs reach through that last sliver of silicon to the transistors (imec's test vehicle: 320 nm deep at 200 nm pitch). Intel forms its nano-TSVs from the front, one in every standard cell, and exposes them when it thins; TSMC's A16 instead lands a contact on each transistor's source from below.
  6. Power metal and bumps. A few thick, wide copper layers are built on the back, then the bumps that bring current in from the package.

Why it matters

  • Wires get more resistive as they shrink, so power that drops through 15 or more thin layers loses voltage on the way. Feeding it from a few thick layers directly underneath keeps the supply steady, so the chip can clock higher or run at lower voltage.
  • With power gone from the front, the fine layers are free for signals: standard cells pack more tightly and the smallest metal pitch can relax, which helps cost and yield.
  • Two designs compete. Intel's PowerVia drops a nano-TSV into every cell; TSMC's Super Power Rail contacts the source directly, which costs more process steps but saves more area. Intel pairs PowerVia with RibbonFET on Intel 18A for up to 15% better performance per watt and 30% density over Intel 3.
  • For AI accelerators the gain is mostly power: hundreds of amps arrive at well under a volt, and every millivolt lost in the wiring is margin the chip cannot use for speed.
