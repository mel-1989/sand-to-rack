Inductively coupled plasma etch chamber

Plasma etch

Lithography only draws the pattern in a thin layer of resist. Etching carves it into the film underneath, and it has to cut straight down, not sideways. A plasma etcher does this with a glowing gas of ions and reactive fragments, inside a vacuum chamber like this one.

What happens

  1. Pump down. A robot sets the wafer on the electrostatic chuck, the slit closes, and the turbo pump takes the chamber from atmosphere to a high vacuum. The throttle valve then holds the working pressure, 2 to 100 mTorr on a typical ICP tool, while gas flows in.
  2. Gas in. Flow controllers in the gas box meter the etch gases (fluorocarbons for oxide, chlorine or HBr for silicon and metals) through the injector in the window's centre.
  3. Strike the plasma. RF at 13.56 MHz in the flat coil on top induces a current in the gas through the ceramic window. Electrons gain energy, split molecules into reactive radicals and ionise them: a dense plasma, about 10¹¹–10¹² cm⁻³.
  4. Bias on. A second RF supply on the chuck makes the wafer charge negative, so a thin dark sheath forms just above it. Ions fall through it and hit the wafer almost straight down. The coil sets how many ions there are; the bias sets how hard they hit.
  5. The trench deepens. Radicals react with the exposed film; the ion bombardment drives that reaction on the trench floor. On the walls, where few ions land, a thin polymer or oxide layer builds up and protects them, so the cut stays vertical (anisotropic).
  6. Endpoint. The spectrometer watches the plasma's light through the viewport. When the film clears, the products of that reaction stop forming and their emission lines fade; for an oxide etch in fluorocarbon gas the CO line at 451 nm drops, and the tool stops.
  7. Strip. An oxygen plasma ashes the remaining resist and the sidewall polymer, often in the same chamber or a neighbouring one, before the wafer goes on to clean.

Why it matters

  • Two RF supplies, one for density and one for ion energy, let engineers tune how fast and how straight a tool etches almost independently. That split is what made ICP the workhorse for gates, fins and shallow trenches.
  • Memory pushes etch hardest: 3D NAND channel holes now run more than 50 times deeper than they are wide. Lam's third-generation cryogenic etch, which etches at very low temperature, claims profile deviation under 0.1% of depth and 2.5× the rate of conventional dielectric etch.
  • At the newest nodes, atomic layer etching removes material in self-limiting cycles of a few atoms each, trading speed for control. Lam lists plasma-enhanced ALE on its Kiyo conductor etch line.
  • The edge of the wafer is where yield is lost: replaceable focus rings and sheath tuning at the wafer edge keep the ions vertical all the way out.
