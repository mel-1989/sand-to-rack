Ion implanter

Ion implantation and anneal

A transistor works only where the silicon has been seeded with a few foreign atoms: boron makes it p-type, phosphorus or arsenic n-type. An ion implanter fires those atoms into the wafer as a beam, choosing the exact element with a magnet, the depth with the beam energy and the amount by counting charge. A short, very hot anneal then heals the damage and lets the atoms take their place in the crystal.

What happens

  1. Ionise. A dopant gas (BF₃, PH₃ or AsH₃) flows into the arc chamber, where a hot filament strikes a plasma of charged fragments.
  2. Extract. Electrodes at high voltage pull positive ions out through a slit, forming a beam.
  3. Select the mass. In the analyzer magnet each ion bends on a radius set by its momentum, so at one field strength only one mass (say ¹¹B⁺, not ¹⁰B⁺ or BF₂⁺) lines up with the resolving slit. The rest strike the walls. The readout's Bρ (field times bend radius) is what the magnet must supply; heavier ions need more.
  4. Accelerate. A column of graded electrodes (or an RF linear accelerator for MeV beams) sets the final energy, which sets the depth.
  5. Scan. The beam sweeps side to side while the wafer moves through it, tilted about 7.4° so ions cannot slip down the crystal's open channels. A Faraday cup at the edge of the sweep measures beam current; integrating it gives the dose.
  6. Stop at depth. Each ion loses energy in collisions and stops in a spread around a mean depth, the projected range. Heavier ions stop shallower.
  7. Anneal. The implant knocks the crystal about. A spike anneal in a lamp-heated chamber, or a millisecond laser or flash anneal, repairs the lattice and moves the dopants onto lattice sites, where they become electrically active, while letting them diffuse as little as possible.

Why it matters

  • Implantation separates the two things a designer needs: how many atoms (the dose, counted precisely from the beam current) and how deep (the energy). Diffusion from a surface source could not do that.
  • As transistors shrank, junctions had to get shallower, so beams went to lower energies and anneals got shorter: from furnace hours, to lamp spikes of about a second, to millisecond laser and flash anneals that heat only the surface.
  • Image sensors and power chips need deep wells, which pushed high-energy tools to RF linear accelerators and energies of several MeV.
  • One modern logic chip goes through many implants (wells, threshold adjusts, source/drain extensions, contacts), each one a separate trip through a machine like this.
