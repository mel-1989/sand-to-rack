Five GPU packages side by side

Accelerator packages, V100 to Rubin

Nine years of NVIDIA's data-center GPUs, laid side by side to scale. A single die barely grew, because no die can be larger than the lithography field; what changed is everything around it: denser transistors, more and faster stacked memory, smaller number formats, and finally two dies working as one. Press Play and the inspection gantry visits each package in turn.

What happens

  1. V100 (2017, Volta). One 815 mm² die on TSMC 12 nm FFN with 21.1 billion transistors, beside 4 HBM2 stacks on a CoWoS-S interposer. New: Tensor Cores, small units that multiply whole 4 × 4 tiles of FP16 numbers at once, for 125 TFLOPS. NVLink 2 links GPUs at 300 GB/s.
  2. A100 (2020, Ampere). Almost the same die area (826 mm²) on TSMC N7, but 54.2 billion transistors. Six memory sites, five live and one a silicon spacer that keeps the package flat. New: TF32, so FP32 code runs on Tensor Cores unchanged, and 2:4 structured sparsity, which skips two of every four weights to double throughput (312 → 624 TFLOPS FP16).
  3. H100 (2022, Hopper). 814 mm² on TSMC 4N, 80 billion transistors, 5 live HBM3 stacks at 3.35 TB/s. New: the Transformer Engine, which chooses between FP8 and 16-bit math layer by layer and rescales values so 8-bit numbers keep enough accuracy: 1,979 TFLOPS dense FP8.
  4. B200 (2024, Blackwell). A single die had hit the reticle limit, so B200 uses two reticle-sized dies joined by a 10 TB/s die-to-die link (NV-HBI) and presented to software as one GPU: 208 billion transistors on TSMC 4NP, 8 HBM3e stacks, 8 TB/s, on CoWoS-L; memory, bandwidth and FLOPS here are per-GPU shares of NVIDIA's DGX B200 totals. New: FP4 with fine-grained (micro-tensor) scaling, about 9 PFLOPS dense.
  5. Rubin (2026). Two dies again, now on TSMC N3P with 336 billion transistors, and 8 HBM4 stacks with twice the interface width per stack: 288 GB at 22 TB/s. NVLink 6 reaches 3.6 TB/s per GPU and NVFP4 throughput is quoted at 35 PFLOPS (training) to 50 PFLOPS (inference).

Key innovations

  • Matrix units (Tensor Cores, 2017). Neural networks are mostly matrix multiplies, so dedicated tile multipliers gave far more math per watt than general-purpose cores.
  • Smaller numbers (FP16 → TF32 → FP8 → FP4). Halving the bits per number roughly doubles the math per cycle and halves memory traffic. Scaling tricks (the Transformer Engine, micro-tensor scaling) keep training and inference accurate. This is also why the FLOPS line is not a like-for-like comparison.
  • Sparsity (2020). A fixed 2:4 pattern of zero weights lets the hardware skip half the work without irregular memory access.
  • Memory beside the die. Every generation added HBM bandwidth because most AI work waits on memory, not arithmetic. Stacks grew from 4 to 12 DRAM dies, and HBM4 doubled the interface to 2,048 bits.
  • Two dies as one (2024). At the reticle limit, the only way to grow is more dies with a link so fast (10 TB/s on B200) that software sees one GPU. NVLink then joins many such GPUs into one rack-scale system.
