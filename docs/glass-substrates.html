Glass core panel with through-glass vias

Glass-core substrates

Every accelerator package sits on a substrate: a thick board that carries power and signals from the circuit board up to the chiplets. Today its core is a glass-fibre and resin laminate. The next step is a core of solid glass, which stays flat and keeps its size when hot, so much bigger packages become possible, and its holes (laser-modified, then etched) can sit up to about 3.2 times closer together, so more wires fit through the core. Press Play to follow one 510 × 515 mm panel from bare glass to finished packages.

What happens

  1. Bare panel. A thin sheet of glass, 510 × 515 mm (the panel size DNP is scaling to), is held flat on a vacuum chuck. Glass is very flat to begin with, which Intel says improves depth of focus for lithography, and its thermal expansion is set by its composition (about 3 to 9 ppm/°C), so it can be tuned toward silicon (about 3) or toward the board.
  2. Laser modify. The table steps the panel under a laser head row by row. A single pulse at each via site changes the glass through its whole thickness (up to 1 mm) without cutting it, so no cracks or chips form.
  3. Wet etch. In a wet bath the laser-changed glass dissolves much faster than the rest, and every site opens into an hourglass-shaped through-glass via (TGV). LPKF quotes aspect ratios of 1:10 to 1:50.
  4. Seed and fill. A thin seed metal coats the hole walls and copper (Cu) is plated in, turning each hole into a vertical wire through the core.
  5. Build-up layers. Fine wiring layers (redistribution layers, RDL) are built up on both faces. Because glass stays flat and does not shrink, the layers line up better: Intel targets lines and spaces under 5 µm.
  6. Singulate and attach. The panel is cut into substrates (four of the largest, 240 mm, fit on one panel) and the compute dies and memory stacks are bonded on top. In a factory these last steps run on other machines; the figure keeps the panel on one stage.

Why glass, and who is building it

  • Organic cores bow and shift as they heat, which caps how fine the wiring can be and how big a package can get. Intel gives four reasons for glass: higher temperature tolerance with 50% less pattern distortion, ultra-low flatness for lithography depth of focus, dimensional stability for tight layer-to-layer overlay, and up to 10× interconnect density. The section display in front of the machine shows the first three side by side.
  • The laser-then-etch trick (LIDE, from LPKF) is what makes glass practical: drilling glass mechanically or by ablation cracks it, while one pulse plus a wet etch leaves clean, tapered holes in the millions.
  • Intel built a glass R&D line in Chandler, Arizona for over $1 billion (2023). Since then it has reportedly begun licensing its glass-core technology and pushed its own use toward 2030 (trade press reports, not an Intel statement). SKC's Absolics has the first plant, in Covington, Georgia, backed by $75 M of CHIPS production funding plus up to $100 M in R&D grants (NAPMP); it is still in customer qualification rather than volume shipping. Samsung Electro-Mechanics signed a glass-core joint venture with Sumitomo Chemical's Dongwoo Fine-Chem in July 2026, aiming for first production in the second half of 2027.
  • Glass is brittle and its edges can chip, and filling deep, narrow holes evenly is hard. These, and cost, are why organic and glass substrates are expected to coexist for years.
