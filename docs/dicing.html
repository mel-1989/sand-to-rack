Back-grinder and dicing saw

Thinning and dicing

A finished wafer is 775 µm thick, far thicker than a chip needs to be, and it holds dozens to hundreds of chips that are still joined together. Two machines fix that: a grinder wears the back of the wafer away until only a thin skin of silicon under the circuits is left, and a dicing saw cuts along the narrow streets between the chips so each one can be picked off on its own.

What happens

  1. Tape the front. A protective tape is laminated over the circuit side. The wafer is then laid face down on the grinder's porous ceramic chuck, which holds it by vacuum through the tape.
  2. Back-grind. A diamond cup wheel (Φ300 mm on a DISCO DGP8761) spins with its rim crossing the wafer centre while the chuck turns underneath, and feeds slowly downward. A coarse wheel takes most of the stock, a fine wheel the last tens of microns, and a contact gauge stops the feed at the target: here 50 µm, so about 94 % of the silicon is ground away. Grinding leaves a thin layer of cracked, strained silicon behind, so the same tool then polishes it away (dry polish or CMP); without that step a die this thin cracks easily. Tools of this class go below 25 µm.
  3. Mount on a frame. A wafer that thin is as flexible as paper, so it is laminated onto dicing tape stretched across a steel ring frame, and the front tape is peeled off. From here on the frame is what the machines handle.
  4. Align. On the dicing saw a camera finds the pattern of streets, the empty lanes left between chips, and the θ table turns the wafer until they run exactly along the X axis.
  5. Cut X, turn, cut Y. A hub blade a few tens of microns thick spins at tens of thousands of rpm while the table feeds the wafer under it; water jets cool the blade and wash away the slurry. After each street the spindle indexes across by one die pitch (the axis can step by 0.1 µm). When every X street is cut the table turns 90° and the Y streets are cut the same way.
  6. UV release and pick. UV light cures the tape's adhesive so it lets go of the dies. A vacuum collet then lifts the good dies off the tape, helped by an ejector needle pushing up from underneath; dies flagged bad in the wafer probe map are left behind. In a fab this is done on a separate die sorter or bonder, after the tape is stretched to open gaps between the dies.

Why it matters

  • Thin dies are what make stacking possible. An HBM stack piles 12 or more DRAM dies inside a fixed package height, so each die has to be ground to a few tens of microns; SK hynix made its dies 40 % thinner to go from 8 to 12 high.
  • Through-silicon vias are buried in the front of the wafer. Grinding brings the back to within a few microns of the vias, and an etch or polish then reveals them, so the stacked dies can connect through their backs.
  • Every street is silicon that is thrown away. Thinner blades and narrower streets fit more dies on a wafer; common blades are 20 to 40 µm wide, and DISCO's ZHZZ hub blades can cut a 10 µm kerf.
  • Stealth dicing replaces the blade with a laser focused inside the wafer. It leaves a weakened layer along each street, then the tape is stretched and the dies snap apart: no kerf loss, no water, no chipping. Done before grinding (SDBG), it is aimed at dies under 50 µm, which a blade struggles to cut without cracking.
