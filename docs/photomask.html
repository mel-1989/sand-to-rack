Multi-beam mask writer

From design to photomask

Before a fab can print a chip it needs a stencil for every layer: a photomask. A finished design is turned into data, the data is written onto a glass blank by 262,144 electron beams at once, and the blank is etched, checked and sealed. Press Play to follow one mask from tape-out to pellicle.

What happens

  1. Design to tape-out. Engineers describe the chip in RTL (Verilog). Synthesis maps it to a netlist of standard cells, place and route lays out the cells and wires, and sign-off checks timing, power and the foundry's design rules (DRC, LVS). The result is tape-out: a GDSII or OASIS file of polygons, one set per layer.
  2. Correct and fracture. When features approach the smallest size the scanner's optics can resolve, light diffracts and neighbouring shapes blur into each other, so optical proximity correction (OPC) or inverse lithography (ILT) reshapes every polygon until the printed result matches the design; ILT often produces curves. The corrected data is then fractured into the writer's own format, here pixel data for 262,144 beamlets, streamed at up to 120 Gbit/s.
  3. Write. One cathode lights an aperture plate with 512 × 512 holes; each beamlet is switched on or off per pixel, and 200:1 optics shrink the array to an 81.92 µm field of 20 nm beams at 50 keV. The stage carries the resist-coated blank under the column in vacuum, stripe by stripe. These are the published figures of the first production generation, the IMS MBMW-101 (2016). Later tools push further: IMS's MBMW-201 for the 5 nm node (2019) and MBMW-301 for 2 nm-class masks (2023), and NuFlare's MBM-3000, which doubled its beam count and shrank each beam from 16 to 12 nm.
  4. Develop and etch. The written blank leaves the writer for separate develop and etch tools, shown here as the bench in front. The exposed resist is developed, and the pattern is etched into the TaBN absorber down to the Ru capping layer. Where absorber is removed, the 40-pair Mo/Si mirror below reflects EUV light; where it stays, the light is absorbed.
  5. Inspect and repair. The finished pattern is compared against the design data. Defects are mapped and fixed, for example with a focused electron beam that adds or removes material.
  6. Pellicle. A thin membrane on a frame is mounted over the pattern so dust lands out of focus instead of on the mask. For EUV the target is at least 90 % transmission on each pass; the light crosses the membrane twice, on the way in and on the way out, so about 81 % survives the round trip. The whole pellicle stands no more than 2.5 mm above the mask.

Why it is built this way

  • A single shaped beam writes one rectangle at a time, so dense, curvy ILT patterns could take 30 hours or more. Writing all 262,144 pixels of a field at once makes write time almost independent of pattern complexity.
  • EUV light is absorbed by every material, including glass, so an EUV mask is a mirror, not a transparent stencil: a Bragg stack of molybdenum and silicon layers each a few nanometres thick, with the pattern in an absorber on top.
  • The scanner shrinks the mask 4×, so a mask error is also shrunk, but every defect on a mask is printed in every die of every wafer; that is why each mask is inspected, repaired and sealed under a pellicle.
