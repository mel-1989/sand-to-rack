Die-to-wafer bonder

Die bonding: bumps to hybrid bonds

A finished die is useless until it is joined to something: a package, an interposer, or another die. A die bonder picks each die off its tape, turns it over and sets it down within a few microns, and for hybrid bonds within a fifth of a micron (200 nm), and the newest tools within 100 nm. What has changed since the first gold wire bonds of 1956 is the joint underneath: from a few hundred wires around the edge to many thousands of copper pads per square millimetre, bonded flat.

What happens

  1. Eject. The diced wafer sits on stretched tape in a steel frame. A needle under the tape pushes one die up so a vacuum tool can take it without bending the tape around it.
  2. Pick and flip. The flip unit's pad takes the die face up and turns it over, so its contacts face down. A wire-bonded die skips this: it goes down face up and is wired at its edge afterwards.
  3. Align. The bond head takes the die from the flip unit and stops over an up-looking camera, which reads marks on the die. A down-looking camera on the head reads marks on the target wafer, and the machine corrects the offset.
  4. Bond. The head presses the die down. Solder bumps are reflowed or pressed with a heated collet (thermocompression). A hybrid bond needs no solder: the die is bowed slightly so it touches at the centre first, the oxide surfaces stick at room temperature under at most 15 N, and the air is pushed out to the edges.
  5. Anneal. For hybrid bonds the whole wafer then goes to an oven at 150–400 °C. The oxide bond strengthens and the copper pads, recessed by a few nanometres, expand into each other and fuse.

Key innovations

  • Flip chip (C4). Turning the die over freed contacts from the edge: bumps can cover the whole face, so the number of connections grows with area instead of perimeter.
  • Copper pillars and thermocompression. A tall copper post with a small solder cap holds its shape, so bumps could shrink to 50 µm. That made the silicon interposer (CoWoS) and stacked HBM memory possible.
  • Hybrid bonding. With no solder, there is nothing to squeeze out sideways, so pads can sit a few microns apart. AMD reports under 1/3 of the energy per bit of micro-bumps.
  • From wafer-to-wafer to die-to-wafer. Hybrid bonding first shipped in 2016, joining two whole wafers in Sony's phone image sensors. The new step in 2022 was bonding single dies onto a wafer at 9 µm (AMD 3D V-Cache), which lets a tested good die go on each site. That is why the machine on this page is a die-to-wafer bonder.
  • The cost is cleanliness and flatness. One particle leaves a void, so hybrid bonders sit in front-end cleanrooms, wafers are polished flat to a few nanometres, and placement must stay well under a micron.
